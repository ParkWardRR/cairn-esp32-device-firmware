# Chasing deep sleep misses the real BMW hazard

Deep sleep is technically reachable on this board and almost entirely pointless: against the Freematics ONE+ Model B's **\~10 mA board floor**, the ESP32's own contribution falls from roughly 0.8 mA in light sleep to ~10 µA in deep sleep, a change of **0.1% of the total budget**. Cairn v2 already light-sleeps, already powers down WiFi and GNSS, and already sends the co-processor into ATLP — so the remaining deep-sleep upside is a rounding error, and the engineering effort would be better spent elsewhere. The elsewhere is specific and BMW-shaped. On an F32 the OBD-II connector carries **D-CAN only**, a diagnostic stub whose sole mandatory participant is the **FEM/BDC gateway** ([Energy Management, the bimmer pub, July 2016, p.8](https://automotivetechinfo.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/BMW-energy-management-systems.pdf)); any dongle that sends an OBD request while parked is waking the body domain controller and everything behind it. BMW's energy management is purpose-built to detect exactly that, classifying it as an **unauthorized wake-up** and, if persistent, a **sleep mode preventer** ([ibid., pp.9–10](https://automotivetechinfo.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/BMW-energy-management-systems.pdf)). Cairn's 5-minute standby threshold happens to land inside BMW's **8-minute first sleep phase**, so the initial park is probably clean — but the **6-hour heartbeat produces four unauthorized FEM/BDC wake-ups per day, each followed by five minutes of 2-second OBD polling**, which is the single behaviour most likely to put a fault flag in the car's energy history. The good news: the firmware's voltage read is bus-silent, which makes a genuinely zero-traffic parked mode achievable without new hardware.

## Deep sleep buys 0.1% against a 10 mA floor, so feasibility barely matters

Start with the arithmetic, because it settles the question before feasibility enters. Espressif's power-mode table for the classic ESP32 gives **light sleep at 0.8 mA**, **deep sleep with RTC timer and RTC memory at \~10 µA**, and **deep sleep with the ULP powered at \~150 µA** ([Lucidar: Power consumption of ESP32](https://lucidar.me/en/esp32/power-consumption-of-esp32/)). Freematics claims the assembled Model B draws "around 10 mA" with GPS, cellular and WiFi powered off ([Freematics ONE+ Model B](https://freematics.com/products/freematics-one-plus-model-b/)). Hold the ~10 mA floor fixed and the whole decision collapses: the board draws **240 mAh/day** at the floor, light sleep adds **19.2 mAh/day** on top (8%), and deep sleep would add **0.24 mAh/day** (0.1%). Swapping light sleep for deep sleep is a 0.8 mA argument against a 10 mA problem. The floor is set by things the ESP32 cannot switch — the co-processor in whatever residual state ATLP leaves it, the CAN/OBD transceiver, the 12V→5V and 5V→3.3V regulator quiescent currents, and any always-on indicator LED. Notably, the Freematics codebase contains **no API of any kind to cut power to the co-processor** — only `ATLP` (sleep request) and `ATR` (reset) over the serial link, and because the ESP32 must wake it by sending bytes down that same link, the co-processor's serial receiver cannot be unpowered ([FreematicsOBD.cpp](https://github.com/stanleyhuangyc/Freematics/blob/master/libraries/FreematicsPlus/FreematicsOBD.cpp)). That is a hard floor no amount of ESP32-side work removes.

Cairn v2's existing standby is also the right architecture for an independent reason that has nothing to do with current. Deep sleep on ESP32 is a reboot: "the CPUs, most of the RAM, and all digital peripherals that are clocked from APB_CLK are powered off," with only the RTC controller, ULP, and RTC FAST/SLOW memory surviving ([ESP-IDF Sleep Modes](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32/api-reference/system/sleep_modes.html)). PSRAM contents are lost too — it sits on VDD_SDIO and is powered down before deep sleep, which is also why WROVER and WROOM have identical deep-sleep current ([Espressif staff, esp32.com](https://esp32.com/viewtopic.php?p=16700)). Every wake would mean re-running the co-processor link bring-up, GNSS reacquisition, and any in-RAM buffer reconstruction. Light sleep preserves all of it, which is exactly why `src/power_standby.cpp` notes that waking "costs nothing but a few milliseconds."

On feasibility, honestly: deep sleep with a hardware wake-on-motion interrupt is *possible* but requires board rework, and the cheaper routes are closed. The classic ESP32's RTC-capable GPIOs are **0, 2, 4, 12–15, 25–27, 32–39** ([ESP-IDF Sleep Modes](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32/api-reference/system/sleep_modes.html)), and cross-referencing the ONE+ Model B pin map leaves exactly two unclaimed: **GPIO36 (SENSOR&#95;VP) and GPIO39 (SENSOR&#95;VN)**. GPIO37/38 are RTC-capable on the die but are not bonded out on WROOM/WROVER modules. Both survivors are input-only with no internal pulls, so the ICM interrupt would have to be reconfigured from its default **pulsed, open-drain, active-low** behaviour to push-pull, active-high and **latched** — all three are software-settable ([TDK AN-000173, p.5](https://www.cdiweb.com/datasheets/invensense/AN-000173-ICM-426xx-Motion-Functions-Description-and-Usage-v1.pdf)) — and paired with `ext1`/`ESP_EXT1_WAKEUP_ANY_HIGH`. The default pulsed mode is simply wrong for ext0/ext1, which are level-triggered; a pulse arriving mid-sleep-entry is lost. All of this presupposes a fly-wire from the ICM's INT pad to a WROVER pad, and **direct inspection of the vendor board header confirms no IMU interrupt pin is routed**, so rework is unavoidable. Two further traps: WROVER has a documented deep-sleep leakage path through GPIO12's external pull-up fighting the chip's internal pulldown, whose official mitigation is `rtc_gpio_isolate(GPIO_NUM_12)` ([ESP-IDF](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32/api-reference/system/sleep_modes.html)) — and on this board GPIO12 is **GPS&#95;POWER/MOLEX&#95;VCC**, so isolating it means surrendering GPS rail control across sleep. There is also a known rev-1 erratum family touching GPIO36/39 behaviour that was not resolved in this research and would need checking in "ESP32 ECO and Workarounds for Bugs" before trusting those pins as wake inputs.

The ULP alternative is a dead end, and worth stating plainly so nobody re-litigates it. Classic ESP32 does have an RTC_I2C peripheral with `I2C_RD`/`I2C_WR` ULP instructions ([ESP-IDF ULP FSM instruction set](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32/api-reference/system/ulp_instruction_set.html)), but it is hard-wired to just two pin selections — **SCL=GPIO4/SDA=GPIO0, or SCL=GPIO2/SDA=GPIO15** ([wardjm/esp32-ulp-i2c](https://github.com/wardjm/esp32-ulp-i2c)). On this board those four pins are LED, boot strap, LINK_SPI_CS and LINK_RESET/GPS_POWER2; two of them are the OBD link's lifeline. Worse, the peripheral is reported broken: "the I2C hardware in the ULP **samples at the wrong edge**," and "the ULP I2C module has been simplified just a tad too much to make it really useful" ([esp32.com](https://esp32.com/viewtopic.php?p=58795)), which is why two independent community repos exist to work around it. The usual escape — bit-banging software I2C from ULP code over arbitrary RTC GPIOs — fails here precisely because the only free RTC GPIOs are 36 and 39, which are **input-only** and cannot drive a bus. Espressif's supported `ulp_riscv_i2c` component is S2/S3 only.

The genuinely useful positive finding is that **none of this is needed to detect motion**. The ICM-426xx APEX block runs Wake-on-Motion and Significant Motion Detection entirely on-chip with no host involvement and no initialization commands, and its results land in readable interrupt-status register fields (`WOM_X_INT`/`WOM_Y_INT`/`WOM_Z_INT`, `SMD_INT`) in addition to asserting the physical pin ([TDK AN-000173, pp.3, 5, 19–22](https://www.cdiweb.com/datasheets/invensense/AN-000173-ICM-426xx-Motion-Functions-Description-and-Usage-v1.pdf)). Because the result latches into a register, a timer-woken CPU can poll "did anything happen since last time?" at its own cadence and lose no events — detection latency equals the poll period, nothing more. SMD, which requires two WoM events inside a 1 s or 3 s window, is the better primitive for a parked car because it rejects door slams and wind buffeting. Cairn's current 1-second light-sleep poll of accelerometer RMS over I2C is already functionally this design; moving to APEX SMD status polling would cut the I2C traffic and the host-side math without changing the architecture. The one thing to verify empirically is whether the WoM/SMD **status bits** are read-to-clear or whether they track the pin's auto-clearing pulse — AN-000173 documents the pin behaviour but is silent on the register, and a slow poller against auto-clearing bits would miss events.

## The F32's OBD connector is a D-CAN stub that the FEM gates

Here is the reframing that matters more than any sleep-current figure. On F3x the OBD connector carries **D-CAN on pins 6 and 14 at 500 kbit/s**, landing at the **FEM** (connector 8B pins 45/46), inside which the **ZGM** gateway translates between D-CAN and the internal PT-CAN, K-CAN and FlexRay buses ([f30.bimmerpost, snippet-level](https://f30.bimmerpost.com/forums/showthread.php?p=30748289)). D-CAN is a diagnostic bus: it carries **no traffic at all unless a tester is present**. Two consequences follow immediately. A purely passive dongle cannot keep an F32 awake — and it also sees absolutely nothing while the car sleeps, so "passive parked monitoring over OBD" is not a thing on this chassis. And any dongle that does get data while parked must transmit a request, which must wake the FEM/BDC, which is the one module gating access to every other bus. **There is no way to poll a little bit without waking the body domain controller.**

BMW's energy management then treats that wake-up as an event with a name. F-series cars are "Boardnet 2020," for which "the majority of the control modules in the vehicle should assume sleep mode in the first 16 minutes (**8 minutes for Boardnet 2020 vehicles**) after terminal 0," and "the vehicle gateway module checks the vehicle bus system for modules that are communicating/still awake at **5, 10, 15 and 20 minutes** after terminal 0. Any module that is still logged on at the 20 minute interval is designated a **sleep mode preventer**" ([Energy Management, p.10](https://automotivetechinfo.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/BMW-energy-management-systems.pdf)). Separately, "**only two modules are authorized to wake up the vehicle**, the CAS and the vehicle gateway… For Boardnet 2020 vehicles using FEM or BDC, those would be the only authorized wake up modules. **All other modules that wake of the vehicle are considered unauthorized wake ups**" ([ibid., p.9](https://automotivetechinfo.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/BMW-energy-management-systems.pdf)). An external tester on the D-CAN stub is, by construction, in the unauthorized category — and BMW itself documents that the energy-diagnosis test module cannot always attribute the waker, displaying "**unknown control unit**" when it cannot interpret the waking CAN message ([ibid., p.10](https://automotivetechinfo.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/BMW-energy-management-systems.pdf)). That is the diagnostic fingerprint a polling dongle leaves.

The car has a defence, and it is terminal 30F. The **30g&#95;f/30F bistable relays** "are normally on and are only switched off in the event of a fault such as **unauthorized bus wake up**, the start capability limit being reached, **closed circuit current violation**, or **sleep mode preventers**… identified by the vehicle gateway (SGM, KGM, JBE, ZGM, FEM or BDC)." The shutdown "starts with a 10 second reset. After the reset the IBS and/or vehicle gateway will continue to monitor for continued activity. If the condition that caused the reset continues, the 30g_f/30F relays will be opened until the next authorized vehicle wake up and **a fault/information flag will be stored**" ([ibid., p.8](https://automotivetechinfo.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/BMW-energy-management-systems.pdf)). So the realistic bad outcome on an F32 is not a mystery flat battery; it is an energy-diagnosis history showing unauthorized wake-ups, possibly a sleep-mode-preventer entry, a 30F information flag, and — well before any no-start — an "Increased battery discharge while stationary" message, which is itself a power-management *action* (the DME has already shed non-essential consumers) rather than a mere warning ([YouCanic](https://www.youcanic.com/bmw-increased-battery-discharge/)). BMW published no TSB naming aftermarket OBD devices; it built the detection into energy management instead.

The cost of holding the bus awake is where the evidence thins out. The one explicit quantification found states that on BMW, keeping the OBD port awake with tester-present messages "**keeps a lot of systems awake and draws roughly 5A on the 12V bus**," described as "not a good idea to do" ([documentation quoted on the OVMS developer list](https://lists.openvehicles.com/archives/list/ovmsdev@lists.openvehicles.com/thread/YC3BF6GHJUARTKLKLZCA6FH56J2KZIOX/)). That is a second-hand quotation of an unnamed source in an i3 context and should not be treated as an F32 measurement. But the **ratio argument survives even if it is wrong by 5×**: a 1 A woken-vehicle draw is still roughly 12× BMW's own 80 mA discharge-cause figure and more than 100× the dongle's own awake current. A nominal 92 Ah AGM taken from full to a practical 50%-DoD floor yields ~46 Ah, which at 1 A is about **35 hours** and at 5 A about **7 hours**. For calibration on partial wakes, BMW documented 2005-MY BMW ASSIST cars drawing "as high as **500 mA** that last for approximately 2 minutes, occurring every 15 minutes for up to 14 hours after key off" as *normal* telematics behaviour ([SI B61 08 00 attachment](https://5series.net/forums/attachments/e60-discussion-2/107177d1276207762-no-audio-sos-call-system-failure-sbt_micro_power_module.pdf)) — so hundreds of milliamps for a partial domain wake is the right order of magnitude to reason with.

## Cairn's 5-minute standby clears the 8-minute window by coincidence; the heartbeat does not

Now apply this to the actual firmware. Cairn v2's standby is gated by `CAIRN_STANDBY_IDLE_MS = 300000` (`include/config.h:148`) — **five minutes of idle**. BMW's first sleep phase is **eight minutes** after terminal 0. The device therefore stops polling OBD roughly three minutes before the body of the car is expected to be asleep, and well before the gateway's **20-minute** sleep-preventer determination. On the initial park, Cairn is probably clean: the FEM sees a tester go quiet during a window in which modules are still legitimately logging off, and the 5/10/15/20-minute checks find nothing attributable to the dongle. That is a real and favourable finding. It is also **coincidence rather than design** — nothing in the firmware references BMW's sleep schedule — and the **margin is three minutes**, which is not much. Any future change that raises the idle threshold past eight minutes, or any trip-end condition that keeps `cairn_power_standby_blocker()` returning non-NULL (an unsealed capture, pending bundles with the link up), pushes the last OBD request past the 8-minute line and starts the clock toward a sleep-preventer classification. Note also the 2-second OBD cadence (`CAIRN_OBD_PERIOD_MS = 2000`): during those five pre-standby minutes the device issues roughly **150 OBD requests**, each one a tester-present-equivalent keeping the gateway up. Those five minutes are not free, they are just inside BMW's tolerance.

The hazard is the heartbeat. `CAIRN_STANDBY_HEARTBEAT_MS = 21600000` (`include/config.h:164`) wakes the device every **six hours** so a parked unit is distinguishable from a dead one — a sound motivation, implemented in a way BMW's energy management is specifically built to catch. On that wake `cairn_power_should_wake()` returns `CAIRN_WAKE_PERIODIC_HEALTH`, the firmware calls `resetLink()` and `leaveLowPowerMode()` to bring the co-processor back, clears `have_recent_obd` and requests an OBD retry — so it resumes polling the vehicle bus. And because `idle_since_ms` is rebased on wake, the 5-minute idle threshold restarts, so the device then **stays awake a further five minutes** before standing by again. The resulting parked-day profile is:

|  | Per event | Per day |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unauthorized FEM/BDC wake-ups | 1 | **4** |
| Minutes of OBD polling | ~5 | **\~20** |
| OBD requests onto D-CAN | ~150 | **\~600** |

Four wake cycles per 24 hours, each bringing the co-processor's OBD session back up for five minutes, sits against BMW's own list of discharge causes, which includes "excessive vehicle wake ups due to unauthorized bus activity" ([Energy Management, p.5](https://automotivetechinfo.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/BMW-energy-management-systems.pdf)).

**Correction, added after review.** An earlier revision of this section called that "a textbook match", which overstates what the sources establish. The cited material shows what BMW's energy diagnosis is *capable of* identifying; it does not demonstrate that each Cairn PID request on an F32 produces an unauthorized-wake record, still less a sleep-preventer classification or a 30F event. A sleep preventer in BMW's own terms is a module that fails to log off during the defined shutdown process, which is not the same thing as an external tester intermittently waking a bus that has already settled. Each link in the chain — request wakes the FEM, wake is classified unauthorized, repetition yields a stored fault — is individually plausible and collectively unproven for this specific car, firmware state and request pattern. Treat the whole chain as a hypothesis worth designing against, not as established behaviour. It is cheap to avoid and expensive to be wrong about, which is the entire argument for the fix; it is not an argument that the mechanism has been verified. The energy cost is also entirely dominated by the vehicle, not the dongle. Twenty minutes per day of woken bus at even a conservative 500 mA is **\~0.17 Ah/day**; at the quoted 5 A it is **\~1.7 Ah/day** — against the dongle's own ~10 mA floor contributing **0.24 Ah/day**. In other words, at the high end of the plausible range **the heartbeat's vehicle-side cost is roughly seven times the dongle's entire standing draw**, and at the low end it is comparable to it. No amount of ESP32 sleep optimisation touches that number. One more detail worth noting since it cuts the other way: during standby the firmware reads supply voltage every tenth poll (`polls % 10 == 0` in `src/power_standby.cpp`), i.e. roughly every ten seconds, and that read briefly wakes the co-processor link. That is a *dongle-internal* wake, not a bus wake — see below — so it costs some of the board's own current but puts nothing on D-CAN.

## Make the parked device bus-silent, then lengthen the heartbeat

The highest-value change follows directly from a distinction the firmware already embodies but does not yet exploit. `COBD::getVoltage()` reads the OBD connector's +12V rail **at the dongle** — a local measurement through the co-processor, requiring no request onto the vehicle's D-CAN bus. `readPID()` and the OBD polling path do put traffic on the bus. Those two are routinely conflated and they are completely different animals from the FEM's point of view. Cairn already has two bus-silent wake signals: engine voltage (`CAIRN_ENGINE_ON_MV`, checked first in `cairn_power_should_wake()` precisely because "a vehicle starting lifts the rail well before it moves") and accelerometer RMS over I2C. **Together those are sufficient to decide when to bring up an OBD session, which means the device can stay bus-silent indefinitely while parked.** The stock Freematics firmware's own OBD-only fallback uses exactly this primitive — `do { delay(5000); } while (obd.getVoltage() < JUMPSTART_VOLTAGE);` with `JUMPSTART_VOLTAGE 14` ([telelogger.ino](https://github.com/stanleyhuangyc/Freematics/blob/master/firmware_v5/telelogger/telelogger.ino)) — so the mechanism is proven on this hardware. Concretely: keep standby polling voltage and the IMU, and gate *any* `readPID()` traffic behind a confirmed engine-on or confirmed-motion transition. Never poll OBD on a timer while parked.

Second, fix the heartbeat. The purpose — distinguishing a parked device from a dead one — is legitimate, and it does not require touching the vehicle bus. Three options in descending preference. Make the heartbeat **bus-silent**: on a `CAIRN_WAKE_PERIODIC_HEALTH` wake, report voltage, IMU state, storage health and uptime, and explicitly *do not* clear `have_recent_obd` or request an OBD retry. That removes all four daily unauthorized wake-ups while keeping the liveness signal, and it is a small, surgical change to the heartbeat branch. Failing that, **lengthen the interval** — 24 or 48 hours cuts the wake count by 4× to 8× and still answers "is it alive?" for any practical monitoring purpose. Failing that, **disable it** for vehicles on this chassis. Also worth fixing regardless: on a heartbeat wake, do not rebase `idle_since_ms`, so the device returns to standby in seconds rather than holding five minutes of awake time it has no use for.

Third, the behavioural mitigations. **Unplug for long parks** — airport trips, travel, winter layup — which is the consistent recommendation across every source consulted: "removing the OBD connector if the car will be sitting for some time prevents 12V battery problems" ([OBD Auto write-up](https://scithings.id.au/OBD_Auto.pdf)). An OBD extension cable with an inline switch is the low-friction version of this and some users fit exactly that. **Double-lock the F32**, which is cited as shortening the CAS's 30g/30B relay drop and getting the car to a lower-power state faster ([Energy Management, p.7](https://automotivetechinfo.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/BMW-energy-management-systems.pdf)) — free, though it does nothing about a terminal-30-fed dongle waking the gateway. And **measure the thing**: run BMW's SI B61 08 00 ritual (trunk lock turned to simulate a closed lid, hood closed, all doors closed, car locked and alarm armed) with and without the dongle, waiting 30–70 minutes each time, and take the **delta** as the real number ([SI B61 08 00, pp.1–2](https://5series.net/forums/attachments/e60-discussion-2/107876d1276867275-normal-battery-open-circuit-voltage-si-b-61-08-00-closed-circuit-current-measurement.pdf)). A clamp meter on the dongle itself in each firmware state would also, finally, produce the measurement nobody has.

## A 92 Ah AGM on a tender does not care about 10–30 mA; registration matters more

The F32's battery lives in the right-hand side of the trunk with the IBS on the negative post. Sources disagree on capacity: the genuine BMW part commonly listed for the 428i is a **92 Ah AGM, p/n 61216806755** in the DIN H8 / BCI Group 49 case ([FCP Euro](https://www.fcpeuro.com/BMW-parts/428i/Battery/)), while F3x siblings are frequently described as **Group 94R / H7 AGM at \~80–95 Ah** ([tpautorepair](https://tpautorepair.net/what-type-of-battery-does-a-bmw-328i-use/)). The likeliest explanation is that BMW fitted H8 to higher-content and start-stop cars and H7 to the rest, with a tray that accepts both — but that is inference, not a sourced fact. **Read the label on the installed battery**: it states the Ah and whether it is AGM, and that is the number that has to match what the DME has coded.

That label matters for a reason bigger than the dongle. BMW requires battery registration, and the failure mode of skipping it is a charging strategy matched to a battery the DME still believes is five-plus years old — described as both undercharging in normal use and **overcharging an AGM**, with field reports of unregistered batteries dying inside a year ([Rick's Free Auto Repair Advice](https://ricksfreeautorepairadvice.com/register-new-bmw-battery/); [SF BMW Coding](https://www.sfbmwcoding.com/replacing-a-bmw-battery/)). Type as well as capacity has to be coded. The battery was replaced about 1.5 years ago; **confirming it was registered, as AGM, with the correct capacity, is worth more than the entire 10–30 mA question.** A chronically undercharged battery is the condition under which a small parasitic draw actually bites, because harm comes from time spent below the **\~12.4 V sulfation onset threshold** ([CTEK](https://ctek.com/en-uk/blogs/ctek-magazine/how-parasitic-drain-affects-your-car-battery)), not from coulombs at these currents.

On the tender: it does not offset a 10–30 mA draw, it **overwhelms** it. The smallest common maintainers source 0.75–1.5 A, which is **25× to 150×** the draw, so the charger spends essentially all its time in float at the AGM's recommended 13.2–13.5 V storage band ([Victron Energy AGM](https://www.victronenergy.com.au/batteries/agm-super-cycle-battery)). A tender does not make the parasitic load disappear, but it removes the only mechanism by which a load this size causes harm. On an F3x, connect at the engine-bay jump-start posts rather than directly at the battery terminals, so charge current passes through the normal path and the IBS stays in sync — an inference from the shunt topology, worth confirming against the owner's manual. Which leads to the reassuring part: **the IBS will not be confused by this load.** It is a mechatronic sensor on the negative lead "measuring battery voltage in, battery voltage out, current in, current out, and battery temperature under all operating conditions" ([Energy Management, pp.5–6](https://automotivetechinfo.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/BMW-energy-management-systems.pdf)). A dongle fed from the OBD connector returns through the normal chassis path, i.e. **downstream of the shunt**, so its current is measured and correctly integrated into SoC. The claim that an added drain "confuses the IBS" is folklore; the one wiring mistake that genuinely corrupts the model is tapping on the battery side of the IBS, which an OBD dongle by definition does not do. SoH is computed from cranking voltage drop and is unaffected directly — though a chronically lower SoC produces a bigger cranking drop, which the DME reads as worse health, which tightens the start-capability limit. For a car driven weekly, 30 mA costs **0.72 Ah/day**, which a 20-minute drive repays roughly ninefold.

Here is the drain arithmetic, assuming a 92 Ah AGM and a 50% DoD floor, with the honest caveat that this is calculation from cited current figures and not measurement:

| Total closed-circuit current | Ah/day | Days from 100% SoC | Days from a realistic 80% SoC |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 mA (factory best case) | 0.24 | ~190 | ~115 |
| 20 mA (factory upper) | 0.48 | ~96 | ~58 |
| 20 mA + 10 mA dongle | 0.72 | ~64 | ~38 |
| 20 mA + 30 mA dongle | 1.20 | ~38 | ~23 |
| 1 A, bus held awake | 24 | ~2 | ~1 |

The shape of that table is the whole argument. The dongle's own consumption moves the parking window from months to weeks. Holding the bus awake moves it to **days**. Four heartbeat wake-ups per day sit between the two, closer to the first — but they are also the row BMW's gateway logs and acts on, and the only row that can store a fault.

## What nobody has measured, and how much of this rests on snippets

This report should be read with its evidentiary state in full view, because several of the load-bearing numbers are weaker than they look. **No independent current measurement of any Freematics ONE+ variant exists anywhere I could find** — no blog, no teardown, no GitHub issue, no forum post with a meter reading. The 20 mA / 50 mA / ~10 mA figures are **vendor marketing claims** published without test conditions, supply voltage, or firmware version ([Freematics ONE+](https://freematics.com/products/freematics-one-plus/); [Model B](https://freematics.com/products/freematics-one-plus-model-b/)). The Freematics user forum — the natural home for such measurements — is **currently offline**, serving a WordPress maintenance page under a TLS certificate for an unrelated domain, so absence of evidence there is weak evidence of absence. And **nobody has measured this device's current in any state**: there is no meter reading for Cairn v2 awake, in standby, or mid-heartbeat. Every power figure in this report is therefore either a vendor claim, a silicon-level datasheet figure that excludes the GNSS module, co-processor, CAN transceiver and regulators, or arithmetic on top of those. A clamp meter and an afternoon would replace the shakiest third of this analysis with facts.

The vehicle-side figures have their own problems. The **\~5 A bus-awake number is the single weakest load-bearing claim here**: one second-hand quotation of unnamed documentation on a developer mailing list, in a **BMW i3** context, which is a different platform (I01, EV) with EV-specific power management. It should not be transferred to an F32 as a measurement. The reason the conclusion survives is that the argument is a **ratio**, not an absolute — at 1 A instead of 5 A the heartbeat still costs the vehicle more than the dongle costs itself, and still exceeds BMW's 80 mA discharge-cause figure by an order of magnitude. BMW forum sources were **largely inaccessible**: bimmerpost returned HTTP 403 and bimmerfest redirected to a paywall proxy (HTTP 402), so every bimmerpost/bimmerfest claim here is **search-snippet level** and should be re-verified by a human with browser access before being relied on. That includes the F30 D-CAN-lands-at-FEM-8B-45/46 detail, which is plausible and consistent but snippet-sourced.

Two numeric distinctions deserve explicit care. First, **BMW's published closed-circuit-current specification stops at F01/F02/F07 at 7–21 mA after 30 minutes**; SI B61 08 00's last revision is January 2010 and predates the F3x entirely, and **no F3x-specific figure was found** ([SI B61 08 00, p.2](https://5series.net/forums/attachments/e60-discussion-2/107876d1276867275-normal-battery-open-circuit-voltage-si-b-61-08-00-closed-circuit-current-measurement.pdf)). Reading 7–21 mA across to the F32 is the conservative extrapolation, and it matters because it means a 20–30 mA added load is not "a small fraction of the allowance" — it is plausibly **one to four times the car's entire factory sleep current**. Second, the **50 mA and 80 mA figures are different things and are routinely conflated**. The 50 mA line is SI B61 08 00's blanket trigger for *investigation* across all chassis; the 80 mA figure is the trade press describing what energy diagnosis treats as a *cause of discharge* ([Energy Management, p.5](https://automotivetechinfo.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/BMW-energy-management-systems.pdf)). Quoting 80 mA as "the BMW spec" overstates the allowance for an F-chassis car. Beyond those, several specifics remain genuinely unresolved: no F3x wiring diagram or fuse designation for the OBD socket supply was found, so pin 16 being on terminal 30 rather than 30B is high-confidence inference rather than primary fact; no source states how long an F3x stays awake after a *single* diagnostic request before restarting its sleep sequence; no ICM-42627 datasheet could be located, so every register address from AN-000173 must be verified against the actual part before use; no residual co-processor current after `ATLP` is documented anywhere; and no manufacturer or BMW study quantifying battery-life reduction as a function of added parasitic current appears to exist publicly.

## Conclusion

The two questions turned out to have inverted answers. Deep sleep — the one the firmware could plausibly implement — is the question that does not matter, because the board's unswitchable floor is a thousand times the ESP32's deep-sleep current and the co-processor cannot be powered off at all. Battery drain — the one that sounds like a current-consumption problem — turns out not to be about the dongle's current either. On an F32 the dongle's own draw is a weeks-scale effect that a tender erases entirely, while its *bus traffic* is a days-scale effect the car actively polices. The correct mental model for this device is not "how little can it draw" but "how rarely does it speak," and the firmware is already much closer to the second goal than the stock Freematics firmware ever was — which busy-polls the IMU at full clock while servicing BLE and, in WiFi builds, stays associated throughout "standby" ([telelogger.ino](https://github.com/stanleyhuangyc/Freematics/blob/master/firmware_v5/telelogger/telelogger.ino)).

What that reframing buys is a cheap fix for a real problem. The 5-minute standby threshold landing inside BMW's 8-minute window is good luck that should be written down as a constraint before someone changes it. The 6-hour heartbeat is a deliberate feature whose cost was invisible until the D-CAN gating was understood, and it can be made free by keeping the heartbeat bus-silent — report voltage, IMU and storage health, skip the OBD retry. The deeper design principle is the one the voltage-versus-PID distinction exposes: this device has two sensing channels that the FEM cannot see (the local +12V rail and the accelerometer) and one it always can (D-CAN). Build the parked-mode state machine entirely out of the first two, bring up the third only on a confirmed drive transition, and the dongle becomes something that can honestly be left plugged in. Until somebody puts a meter on it, though, that is a well-reasoned design argument rather than a verified result — and the meter reading is the cheapest unresolved item on the list.
